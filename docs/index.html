RSA by hand

A worked example of RSA encryption with small numbers. Change p, q, e or the message and everything below is recomputed. Code is on GitHub.

RSA lets someone send you a message that only you can read, even though you never met to agree on a password. It's used whenever your browser connects to a site over HTTPS. All of it comes from a few results in number theory: Euclid's algorithm, modular inverses and Euler's theorem.

Step 1. Pick two primes

Real keys use primes around 300 digits long. These are small so the arithmetic fits on a page.

Step 2. Multiply them

n is public. p and q stay secret. The point of RSA is that recovering p and q from n (factoring) is much harder than multiplying them.

Step 3. Compute φ(n)

φ(n) is Euler's totient: how many numbers from 1 to n share no factor with n. When n is a product of two different primes, it works out to (p − 1)(q − 1). You need p and q to compute it, so it stays secret too.

Step 4. Choose e

e has to share no factor with φ(n), otherwise step 5 is impossible. Check with Euclid's algorithm, gcd(φ(n), e):

Step 5. Find d

d is the inverse of e mod φ(n), meaning e × d leaves remainder 1 when divided by φ(n). The extended Euclidean algorithm finds it: do the same divisions as in step 4, but write each remainder as a combination of φ(n) and e.

Why decrypting works

From step 5, e × d = 1 + k × φ(n) for some whole number k . So

cd = med = m1 + kφ(n) = m × (mφ(n))k ≡ m × 1k = m (mod n)

The step mφ(n) ≡ 1 (mod n) is Euler's theorem. It needs m and n to share no factor. RSA still works when they do, but proving that takes the Chinese Remainder Theorem.

Breaking it

Someone listening in knows n = and e. If they can factor n, they can repeat steps 3 to 5 and get d. The simplest method is to divide by 2, 3, 5, 7, … until one works.

Trial division needs up to √n tries, so each extra digit of n makes it about 3 times slower. For a real 617-digit n that's around 10308 tries. Faster factoring algorithms exist, but at that size they're still far too slow.

This is "textbook" RSA, the version taught in class. Real implementations add random padding before encrypting, so please don't use this to protect anything. The Python version works with real-size keys and also has signatures, faster decryption and some attacks.
